Title: Tower Control

High-Level Concept:
A calm, tense approach-control game. You are the controller working a radar
desk at a regional airport at dusk. You never leave the desk and you are never
in an aircraft — everything you do, you do with the mouse: you read the strips,
you drag them between bays, and you click the headings. The game is the console.

The Desk
The whole game is one screen made of panels, arranged the way a real position
is laid out. On the left, an inbound queue: a vertical stack of paper flight
strips, one per aircraft, each showing a callsign, a type and a requested
level. In the middle, the radar repeater — a dark square panel with slow sweep
lines and a scattering of contact blips, each one labelled. On the right, a
clearance pad: a column of large square buttons the controller presses to issue
an instruction. Along the bottom, a strip bay divided into three racks —
Inbound, Cleared, Handed Off — that strips are physically moved between. Above
everything, a thin status header showing the active runway, the wind and the
current pressure setting.

What the Player Does
Pick up a flight strip from the inbound queue and drop it into a rack. Click a
contact on the radar to select it, then press a clearance button to issue the
instruction: descend to level, turn onto a heading, reduce speed, cleared to
land. Each accepted clearance updates that aircraft's strip and moves its blip
one step along its route. There is no character, no vehicle, no camera to fly
— the pointer is the only thing that moves.

The Pressure
Aircraft arrive on a schedule. Each one has a level and a distance, and two
aircraft may not be cleared to the same level while their blips are converging.
When two contacts get too close the pair flashes amber and a conflict card
appears on the desk; clearing one of them to a different level resolves it and
the card is dismissed. A strip left in the Inbound rack too long turns red.

Shift Goal
Work the arrivals bank: get every strip from Inbound to Handed Off with no
conflict card left standing. When the last strip is handed off, the header
prints the shift score and the desk goes quiet.

Look and Feel
Muted, low-contrast, instrument-panel colours — dark slate panels, amber and
pale green text, a single warm desk lamp. Everything is flat and readable.
Panels have real edges and sit slightly proud of the desk surface so the layout
reads at a glance. No 3D scenery of any kind; the airport exists only as marks
on the radar and words on the strips.

Audio
A low room tone with the hum of the radar. A soft paper sound when a strip is
picked up and a firmer one when it is racked. A single soft chime on an
accepted clearance and a two-tone alert when a conflict card appears.
